The same two days as a story, in my own words: I had two days to build an Unreal Engine game with Claude (Medium).

The Glass Box book (24 pages). My own prompts with their times, what the agent did with each one, what the tests showed, and what is still wrong. Read the book on the web or download the PDF.

The short version

I made CUT! AGAIN! for the Epic AI MegaJam in about 30 hours: from 07:05 on Saturday 14 November to the submission at about 13:00 on Sunday 15 November. I found the jam with two days left. I directed and played; an AI coding agent, Claude, wrote the code. This is the short version. The full log the agent kept while it worked is at the bottom, as it was written. All times are Gulf time (UTC+4).

At a glance

  • About 30 hours from an empty C++ project to the submission, with two restarts on the first morning.
  • Three scenes, ten kinds of wreck, a score that is the film's profit, and a world leaderboard.
  • Shipping builds numbered up to H14, the one I submitted: a 557 MB zip against the 1 GB limit.
  • The game saves its own videos to share: the whole film wide and vertical, and a short for each wreck.
  • 71 log entries, written by the agent as it worked. They are all at the bottom of this page.

Who did what

  • Me: the idea, every decision about the game, and playing each build and sending it back with notes and marked-up screenshots. I wrote no code.
  • Claude (Anthropic), in Claude Code: all game code (C++ in Unreal Engine 5.9), the editor scripts in Python, the test tools, the build scripts, and the pipeline that cuts the trailer from recordings of the game.
  • Codex (OpenAI): the first engine set-up on 13 November, and the images for the game's social pages.
  • ElevenLabs: the director's voice, the sound effects and all music, generated during the jam. Engine and horn sounds come from a short numpy script.
  • Epic: Lucky's body and everything that moves it (Game Animation Sample 5.8.2: motion matching, vaults, ledge grabs, ragdoll), the sports car and the sky. Every other set piece, the truck included, is built in code from cubes and cylinders.
  • Unreal MCP: switched on and tried on a test project, but nothing in this game was built through it.

How I worked with the agents

  • One Claude Code session talked to me, planned, and checked results by looking at the pictures the game saved. It wrote a contract file for the builder: the game on one page, milestones with clock times, stop points where I play the build, and rules.
  • The builder was a separate Claude agent (Opus, high effort) that started fresh from that file, kept a progress file for whoever came next, and wrote the log at the bottom of this page. It built the whole 2D game in 47 minutes and the first rough 3D build in about 50.
  • It would not act on two of my instructions when they reached it through the planner instead of from me (skip a release check, put my name in the credits): it ran the check anyway and left the credit line empty.
  • Other jobs went to their own sessions with their own written briefs: the leaderboard server, the videos, the article, and the tester.

Lines from the builder's contracts (07:00 and 08:50 on Saturday):

The player must be doing something, not watching. Short takes, fast retries.

If G2 is not playable by Saturday 15:00, stop and report. We then return to the 2D game.

State failures plainly. A wrong "it works" costs us more hours than an honest "it does not".

The same plan said "Never let raw physics decide whether a take passes." After my play at 10:15 we did the opposite: no table decides a take now, Lucky's own movement does.

From the brief for the game's side of the leaderboard: "Check your signer against the test vector before touching the network." The game's signature matched it to the byte.

How an agent that cannot see or hear checked its work

  • The game runs on a fixed 60 Hz step, so a scripted run plays the same, frame for frame, every time.
  • Scripts press keys and drag pieces through the real input path, and the game saves its own frames. Every visual claim was checked on contact sheets and full-size frames.
  • Numbers come from measured runs: he sprints at 5.9 m/s, a level leap clears 5.3 m, and the sample's ledge catch saves him across gaps up to 7.5 m. The hint marks were sized from sweeps of real takes.
  • Sound was only measured (tempo, peaks, loudness). The agent never heard the game; only I have.
  • Network code was tested from packaged builds against a local copy of the server.
  • A separate tester session played builds by script. It was allowed to test and report, not to fix, and twice it found bugs that the builder had missed.

The hardest problems

The body that would not fall (Saturday 18:30)

What I saw: a car hit Lucky and he stayed on his feet, bent over the bumper, as if someone had gently pushed him.

The cause: the sample's ragdoll is an active one. It drives every limb toward a pose and holds him upright, so he flew a metre through the air standing up and landed on his feet.

The fix: switch those controls off at the hit and throw the whole body as one, 6.5 m down the road for the car and 8.5 m for the truck. He lands, rolls and lies still. On Sunday the same showed up on a fall beside the mat, so now every wreck goes limp.

The hint that led into a wreck (Sunday 06:50)

What I saw: I put the blue mark on the orange box, as the hint said, and the truck hit Lucky.

The cause: the blue mark was the whole 5 m gap under the trailer and the box only 2.2 m, so "mark on box" held from 1.5 m too early to 1.5 m too late. The slide only works from 1.3 m too early on.

The fix: sweeps of truck and car starts measured the window that really works, and the mark and the box now both have that length. The edges of the window were played in both scenes and all came out clean.

Saved videos darker than the game (Sunday 01:25)

What I saw: the saved videos were too dark, with black shadows.

The cause: the picture buffer used the wrong gamma, and Lumen and the real-time sky light do not run in the scene capture the export films through.

The fix: a 2.2 gamma target, the sky light captured once and made half again as strong, and screen-space bounce light for the export camera. Before, the darkest quarter of an export frame sat at 1 to 3 against 21 to 56 on the screen. After, the mean is 1.4 percent under the screen.

A one-frame flash in the replays (Sunday 10:00)

What I saw: in a saved video Lucky flashed into a curled pose for a single frame now and then. I called it lag.

The search: a pose signature per frame over 28 wrecked takes, 6,832 limp frames, showed the recording itself was clean. The flash came from the replay, which blended two stored frames in mesh space while the sample had turned his capsule half round.

The fix: blend in world space. After it, 22 wrecked takes and 14 saved videos with no flash. The tester's frame-jump detector, run on my video, had flagged those frames (three of its lines):

JUMP frame   131 t=  4.367
JUMP frame   145 t=  4.833
JUMP frame   147 t=  4.900

Twice an agent explained one of my notes away and a test then showed I was right: the hint boxes and this flash.

Two smaller ones

  • Every score was refused from the packaged game while the editor passed every test. An engine name keeps the spelling of whoever used it first, so "SUCCESS" went out as "Success". Network code is now tested from a package.
  • Two copies of the game saving videos at once destroyed each other's files. Each export now has its own scratch files.

What went wrong and was left

  • When the first take of THE BIG ONE ends in a fall at the foot of the container, the camera loses Lucky behind it. I chose to leave it for the jam.
  • Four small pops in the sample's own animation (scene 2 near 4.8 s, scene 3 near 9.3, 12.0 and 12.9 s).
  • Checked only by me, or by nobody: the sound, and the HUD in a real 3840x2160 window. The BONK and TIMEOUT outcomes never came up in a test.

What existed before the jam

  • The game started from an empty C++ project at 07:05 on 14 November: no gameplay, no assets, no map.
  • Not made by me: Epic's Game Animation Sample, the sports car and sky that ship with the engine, the Limelight font (SIL OFL), and FFmpeg, bundled to encode the videos players save.
  • Mine from before: my general Claude Code set-up and working rules, and the server the leaderboard runs on.

The full hour by hour record (71 entries)

This is the working log of CUT! AGAIN!, kept hour by hour during the jam by the AI coding agent (Claude) that wrote the code under my direction. Where it says "the owner", that is me. I have not tidied it: the dead ends, the wrong guesses and the times I sent the work back are all in it. The entries are only put in order of time. Times are Gulf time (UTC+4).

Saturday 14 November 2026

07:05. Started from an empty C++ project. Removed an unneeded Android file server config section and an empty per-user content folder; disabled that plugin so it does not come back.

07:20. Wrote the core in C++ with an AI coding agent (Claude): a flat card mesh builder (ear clipping, extruded outlines), a jointed shadow puppet with forward kinematics, a stage that poses every card from one plain data struct, and the first shot as a pure function of (take, frame, choice).

07:24. First compile failed on a name clash with an engine class (FPoly) and a unity-build shadowing warning; renamed and it built.

07:27. Materials and the empty map made by an editor Python script, not by hand. First rendered frame saved by the game's own test switch.

07:30. The agent cannot watch a screen, so the game renders itself: one switch saves a frame, another saves every frame of an edited film, a third dumps every verdict, a fourth drives the real menus with fake input. Contact sheets of whole takes are how every visual claim gets checked.

07:33. Measured the cut window from pixels instead of guessing: a probe view paints the painted moon blue, the prop moon green and the stick red, and a script counts them per frame.

07:34. First distant skyline was pure black and swallowed the thief's legs; made it dark blue so the black puppet reads in front of it.

07:37. The whole UI flow runs from a script of fake key presses and saves a screenshot at each step.

07:45. First Shipping package builds in about three and a half minutes and stays up in a 20 second run. A test package from the same cook rendered the whole film, so the cooked assets load.

07:47. The set view floor was invisible: every triangle was wound the wrong way, which only shows under lights. One flag flipped, and the stage, tape marks and shadows appeared.

07:55. Owner playtest of the first build: he could not tell how to play. The game now teaches itself: three picture cards explain the trick before the set, every screen has one plain instruction line with the keys drawn as key caps, the set shows a live inset of what the film camera sees, the first edit is a guided walk that waits for each step, and every flawed verdict says what to change.

08:00. Framing: the film camera never moves, but it now pans and zooms in for the leap on the same curve in both takes, so the trick still lines up while the grab fills the screen. Mitten with a thumb, a tied sack on his back, a long cap brim and nose so he reads in silhouette.

08:05. Audio made with ElevenLabs (voice lines, effects, music) imported by a script and wired to events, with the music ducked under the director's voice. Mix peaks checked offline with numpy and the loudest verdict moments turned down below full scale. A silent film title font (Limelight, SIL OFL) for the cards.

08:10. Set view rebuilt as a reveal: a warm spot pool, thick cards, roofs cut as roofs, braces behind every flat, the film camera on its tripod in front, tape marks on the floor. Audience of three silhouettes with gasp, laugh, groan, head tilt and clapping. Premiere, outtakes reel and credits.

08:15. Re-measured the cut window after the framing change: still 29 to 32. A mouse-only run and a keyboard-only run both reach a printed film.

08:25. Direction change: the owner wants a 3D stunt game built on Epic's Game Animation Sample (motion matching and parkour). The 2D game was packaged as a fallback.

08:35. Copied only Epic's sample content into a fresh project and opened it in UE 5.9 offscreen. The motion matching character, its databases and chooser tables loaded clean; a Python probe ran him forward and triggered two vaults, checked on screenshots.

08:50. Switched to Epic's clean 5.8 Game Animation Sample. In UE 5.9 it opened in three minutes with no errors. Driving the character through injected input (the same path a key press takes), he runs, sprints, climbs blocks, falls into a ragdoll and gets back up, all checked on screenshots.

09:00. New game on Epic's Game Animation Sample: a stuntman who does exactly what the script says, and a director who places props. Added a C++ module; the whole course is spawned in code.

09:05. The stuntman is driven only by injecting the sample's own input actions in code. The player's keys go to our director and never reach the character.

09:12. First surprise: the sample's level blocks ignore the scale they are spawned with; we scale them afterwards. Measured the leap off the 4 m runway three times: the three landings were within 10 cm of each other, about 9 m out, so the landing spot is placed by measurement, not by guess.

09:15. The splat: the ragdoll starts a moment before he hits the ground, with a push forward and down, so he crumples instead of landing on his feet.

09:30. Proved the player's keys cannot steer the stuntman: a script mashes the sample's own movement keys every frame through the real input path, and his run and landing match an untouched run within a few centimetres.

09:45. First Shipping package of the 3D game: 1.2 GB on disk, 1.07 GB zipped. The cook pulled in other sample characters through a costume switcher on the pawn; trimming that is the plan for getting under 1 GB.

10:15. The owner played the one-leap build and said it was not a game yet: one jump and two lettered spots for a mat. He asked for a real stunt run: several parkour moves in a row, a moving car that the stuntman vaults by the hood, and set pieces dragged freely with the mouse instead of lettered slots. We kept the engine work and started the game layer again.

10:40. New rule for the stuntman: he reacts instead of following a script. He leaps where the floor ends, asks the sample's parkour for a move at anything climbable, slides under what is too low, and gets hurt by everything else. No table decides a take any more. If the container stands too far away he really does not reach it.

11:05. A lab mode runs lists of takes from text files, each with its own piece positions and camera, and writes frames and an event log per take. First numbers: he sprints at 5.9 m/s, a level leap clears a 5.3 m gap, and the sample's ledge catch saves him across gaps up to about 7.5 m. Nobody had planned for that catch; it became the first beat of the game.

11:20. The car. It has no collision with him at all. A thin invisible climbable block rides on the hood, the sample's parkour vaults it, and while he is on it we carry the motion warping targets along with the car every frame so his hand stays on the moving hood. Hits are decided from geometry: nose first and he rides the hood, side first and he bounces off the door.

11:30. Every take is recorded as bone poses and played back on a stand-in mesh. That gave us the rewind after every cut and the film at the end with its own cameras and slow motion, without simulating anything twice.

11:35. Dragging with the mouse was checked through the real input path, with a scripted stand-in for the cursor position.

11:45. Dressed the lot in code from cubes and cylinders: a sound stage wall, a brick building with windows, a corrugated container, cones, light stands, a camera on a tripod, and real clouds.

11:55. After a car fail the road shows where the hood was at the moment he arrived, next to the mark where it has to be, and the distance in metres follows the mouse while the car is dragged. The middle of the starts that work came from a sweep of 18 takes.

12:10. A truck built from boxes, with a trailer high enough to slide under while it drives past. Three scenes now: a warm-up, the car, and one take with a tower, an airbag, the truck and the car. The live camera drops behind him when a vehicle is coming and cuts to the far side once he is through.

12:40. Lucky got a yellow crash helmet with a dark visor, placed from the head bone's own axes so it also sits right on the replay double. Found on the way that our ragdoll check never fired: the sample only simulates the bodies below the pelvis, so asking whether the mesh simulates says no. With the right question the impact sounds, the dust and the bounce off a car door finally happen.

12:55. The package was 1.29 GB. An asset registry walk in the editor showed why: the sample pawn's costume switcher points at the sample game mode, which softly lists five other characters. Marking those folders as never cooked brought the folder to about 820 MB, and a scripted play-through of the packaged test build still printed the scene.

13:05. Engines, horns, tyre screech and the thump on the hood are synthesised with numpy from oscillators and filtered noise. One looping engine per vehicle, louder as it nears him, and it plays in the rewind and the film too because those move the vehicles from the recording.

13:10. The cook of the engine preview sometimes dies in a pose search worker thread right after a periodic garbage collection. Not our content: the same cook passes on the next run. The package script now tries again by itself.

13:20. The gameplay video is made by the game. A script plays a scene with a drawn stand-in cursor and saves every second frame at a fixed 60 Hz step, so slow motion looks the way a player sees it. The offscreen run has no audio device, so the game logs every sound it starts with its frame number and a small numpy mixer rebuilds the track, engines included. All three scenes were then played to the end by script on a packaged build.

14:10. The owner asked to see a whole round played, mistakes included. Three scripts play the three scenes the way a player would: watch the fail, read the hint, drag the piece with the mouse, go again. Watching the result caught what no log would have: after a rewind the stand-in's helmet stayed visible and hung in the air at the start mark. The rebuilt sound track also showed the mix going to 1.8 of full scale when a car hit him, so those volumes came down.

14:30. A second look at the same video found a real bug. In the outtakes Lucky fell into the gap and vanished: the film replayed old takes on the set as it stands now, and the container the player had dragged closer since was standing on top of the place where he had landed. Each recorded take now carries the positions of every piece, and a clip puts the set back the way it was.

15:10. The owner watched the play-through and named two holes. The music was a gentle silent film piano under an action scene, and the game had no score. New music from ElevenLabs: a chase track for the take, a trailer track for the film, a funk track for the outtakes that starts on a record scratch, a heist groove for arranging the set. In slow motion the music drops in pitch with a bass sweep and snaps back on the way out, in the live take and in the film.

15:30. The score is the film's profit. Every take costs a day of crew, a wrecked take adds the doctor and the bodywork, and a take the director cuts in time costs only the day, which gives the CUT button a reason. Quality comes from how clean each move was, measured where it happens: how far below the edge he caught the ledge, how far from the middle of the pad he landed, how far from the middle of the hood his hand came down. A take that works can be printed or shot once more for a cleaner one; the film is cut from the best. The owner's own example set the first rule: two takes both made the ledge, but in one his hand went into the wall a metre below the edge.

15:45. Leaderboard: the game posts the scene's profit to a small server (one Python file, standard library, SQLite) and shows the top of the board next to the box office card. The server recomputes the profit from quality and cost and refuses a score that does not add up. Without a server the scores stay on the player's computer.

17:00. Both videos recorded again with the new music and the score. The agent still cannot hear, so the tracks were at least measured: the take music runs near 138 beats a minute and the outtakes near 146, against the old title piano at a third of the brightness. The 57 second video now ends on the box office card: one wrecked take, the fix with the mouse, the clean take, six seconds of the film, then the bill. Watching it showed that a scene's best profit was forgotten when the game closed; it is now saved next to the player's name.

18:30. The owner stopped the play-through on a car hit: the stuntman stayed on his feet, bent over the bumper, as if someone had gently pushed him. The cause was not the size of the push. The sample's ragdoll is an active one that drives every limb toward a flailing pose and holds him upright, so he flew a metre in the air standing up and landed on his feet. For every vehicle hit those controls are now switched off and the whole body is thrown as one: down the road ahead of the car or the truck and out of its path, or onto his back off the side of it. The impact plays at full speed and the slow motion catches him in the air; he lands, rolls and lies still about two seconds after the hit, in the live take, the rewind and the outtakes alike.

19:00. The world board now speaks protocol version 2. A scene asks the server for a session when it opens, the game keeps a log of every take, and the finished film goes out signed with HMAC-SHA256, written into the game because the engine has none on Windows; its signature matches the contract's test vector to the byte. The address can now be changed without a new build, in a settings.txt beside the game. All three scenes were played by script against a local server and arrived with the numbers on the card; with the server stopped the score stayed on the computer. One live post to cutagain.doment.ai as TEST-CLIENT got rank 1 of 1. Found on the way: a take cut at once lasts a fraction of a second, faster than the server's 3 s a take, so the game waits out the server's minimum time before it posts.

19:15. Players can now save their film as videos to share, from a gold button on the box office card: the Director's Cut, the wreck reel and a short clip of each crash, each one wide and vertical, as MP4 with the music. The game does not record its own screen. It plays the film again on its fixed 60 Hz step and two hidden cameras film every second step into pictures of the video's size, wide and vertical at once, while the sounds are written down instead of played and mixed afterwards from copies of the same files, slow motion pitch drop and engine loops included. A bundled ffmpeg turns the pictures and the mix into the files. The 29 second Director's Cut takes about 40 seconds on this machine, faster than it plays.

19:20. The first exported film came out half as bright as the game. A test command filmed one frame of the set through four kinds of picture buffer next to a screenshot: only a 16 bit float buffer matched the screen (median brightness 125 against 123, the 8 bit one 53). The vertical shot was framed on contact sheets: the film cameras look a metre ahead of him, which in the narrow frame put a man lying on the road at its edge, so the vertical camera aims mostly at him; through a car hit both he and the car stay in frame.

19:25. Every video carries a small corner mark with the game's name and "Made with Unreal Engine 5.9", and ends on a three second card with the name, the engine line and the tags to use. 20:00. Checked in a packaged build, not only the editor. The first packaged run was four times slower than the editor: reading the float picture back converted every pixel with a power function. Picture and sound agree within one frame at the end card and at the first slow motion. The same package opened a session on the live leaderboard over HTTPS. The Shipping build H10 is zipped for the jam at 556 MB.

20:20. The submission video and the trailer are cut. The submission video runs 57 seconds and ends on a card that names every borrowed piece and tool; the trailer runs 40 seconds, wide and vertical, and the vertical one takes the film's moments from the game's own vertical videos, matched to the recording by sound and picture to the frame. The game's sound was rebuilt from its log as separate effects, music and voice, so a director's line can finish across a cut. Nobody has listened to either.

Sunday 15 November 2026

01:10. The owner found text soft in places, the instruction line at the top of a take among them. The canvas drew every font at the one size it was loaded at and scaled the glyphs up, so a 28 pixel line at 1080p was a 20 pixel line stretched by half again. Text is now rasterised at the size it is shown, in both typefaces, and placed on whole pixels. On a full size frame of a take the instruction line has twenty times as many hard edges as before.

01:25. The saved videos were too dark, with black shadows. Two causes, found by filming the same frame through the export's camera and the screen side by side. The render target used a display gamma of 1.0, so the picture went through the wrong curve; it now uses the screen's 2.2 and holds exactly the bytes the screen would. The bigger one: Lumen, which lights everything in shadow on the screen, does not run in a scene capture at all, and neither does the real time sky light. During an export the sky light is captured once as a cubemap and made half again as strong, and the export camera uses screen space bounce light. Measured on a frame of THE BIG ONE with deep shadows: mean brightness 1.4 percent under the screen, the darkest tenth of the picture 28 against 24.

01:50. A new set of videos, on the owner's list. One wide film: an opening card with the director's name and the title, the director's cut, a BACKSTAGE card, the wrecked takes with a tenth of a second of television colour bars and a beep between two of them, end credits rolling over the last wreck like the outtakes at the end of an old action film, and a closing card with the name again. Then one vertical short per wreck and one of the director's cut, each opening and closing on the name. The vertical videos are picture from top to bottom now, with the titles over the picture; before, they kept the wide film's black bars. The colour bars and the beep play between the outtakes in the game too.

01:55. Saving waits for a name, because the videos carry it and the owner finds players on the board by it; a name kept from an earlier round counts. The box office card and the saved panel now say it plainly: post it, mention @CutAgainGame, and the studio adds bonus points to that name on the world board.

02:15. Scores from a packaged build never reached the board. The editor happened to spell them right, which is why every earlier test passed. The game now forces the spelling before it signs. Against the reference server on this machine the old test build was refused and the new one was accepted in all three scenes.

02:40. Checked in a packaged build by script at 1920x1080: scene 2 saved its four videos and scene 3 its five, the beeps sit only where the colour bars are, and two moments of the saved film are within 5 percent of the screen's brightness.
